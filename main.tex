\begin{abstract}
We provide a fully analytic, full-spectrum, Ultra High Energy Cosmic Ray (UHECR) reconstruction model. Derived from known Radio Frequency (RF) dipole antenna responses and formulas for the Askaryan effect in antarctic ice. Allowing for a much richer analysis of the radiation's spectrum, extracted electric field, and open the avenue for experimental neutrino flavor physics. Results and implications from running our model on UHECR candidate data is presented in the latter part of the work.

\subsection{Background}

Close to the South Pole, UHECR have been observed to generate electromagnetic cascades in the 0.1-1 GHz Bandwidth through the Askaryan Effect. The Askaryan effect can be understood as a case of the Cherenkov effect. The Cherenkov effect is observed when a particle travels within a dielectric material at velocities faster than the speed of light in the dielectric. This creates a wake of electromagnetic radiation trailing the incident particle. Distinguishably, in the Askaryan effect, runoff cascades of particles are induced in the medium. 
Askaryan cascades also exhibit a geometric nature, analogous to sonic booms. The geometric nature arises from the runoff cascades spherically propagating outwards from the incident's path, we can treat these as instantaneous pulses. If we tangent the extent of each pulse in space, then the tangent of the set of pulses may be understood as the wave front of Askaryan radiation. The wavefront forms an angle, the Cherenkov angle $\theta_c$, to the last known location of the incident particle, denoting the point of a cone. 
Furthermore, because each instantaneous pulse may interfere with another, there arises a \textit{viewing angle} where the radiation is most coherent. In radiation physics, the \textit{viewing angle} $\theta$, is where the given waves most constructively interfere with one another. If $\theta = \theta_c$, and our observation point falls within this angle, then we are \textit{on-cone}. The equality occurs when when $cos(\theta_c) = 1/n_{ice}$ ,and $n_{ice} = 1.78 \pm 0.003$. Which explains why an \textit{on-cone} equation may present an observer with a richer full-spectrum of information.

[[Insert graphic of the Askaryan effect in ice]] \\

The type of induced cascade is determined by properties of the incident UHECR, leading to two observed phenomena. The first is that of a "hadronic" cascade, aptly named so because of the hadrons interacted with. For example, a proton may be the incident UHECR and strike a nucleus, triggering a hadronic cascade. The second case is that of a "Electromagnetic" cascade, in which the cascade is dominated by runoff electromagnetic radiation rather than hadrons. \\

In order to detect the cascades, Askaryan class detectors have been deployed to the antarctic. The antarctic is a prime location for UHECR detection because of its isolation from human induced RF noise, elevation and dielectric composition. Antarctic ice is bored into, and a cable consisting of several dipole antennas is set into position. Each dipole, naturally, has several channels in this case numbered 1 through 7 from highest elevation to lowest. Multiple cables are then connected to a central, semi-autonomous, surface station. RF pulses generated during cascades are recorded by the RF Antenna installations, allowing for study of \textit{off-cone} and \textit{on-cone} radiation. Detectors at the South Pole such as IceCube, the Askaryan Radio Array (ARA) and the Antarctic Ross Ice-Shelf Antenna Neutrino Array (ARIANNA) use RF dipole antennas to detect radiation spikes through the antarctic air and ice. The RF antennas, when shrouded in radiation, emit a "response" function. Thankfully, the required response function has already been identified through the field of digital-signal-processing. If this response is convolved with a function for the interacting radiation, then it is possible to derive the electromagnetic cascade's electric field from the derivative of the convolution. Where by convolution we mean to take the Fourier transform of both functions and multiply the integrals together. From here on, we refer to the product of the convolved functions as the "waveform", see section \ref{sec:Par} for clarification. From our work, we will provide implications from data collected in the above fashion in section \ref{sec:Imp}.  \\
Previous work on a fully analytic \textit{off-cone} radiation model yielded strong correlation between model, simulation and observation. As such we endeavor to demonstrate an \textit{on-cone} model, and comparisons between the two.

\section{Derivation}
\label{sec:Der}
For the Askaryan effect let us denote the radiation at a given time t as a pulse, and treat a set of pulses as signal s(t) then allow $\epsilon = \omega_0/\omega_{\rm C}$.  The signal model is:

\begin{equation}
s(t) = E_0
\begin{cases}
\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right) e^{\omega_0 t} & \text{if } t < 0 \\
2e^{-2\omega_{\rm C}t} - \left(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{-\omega_0 t}  & \text{if } t \ge 0 \label{eq:1}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Let $u(t)$ represent the Heaviside step function to ensure causality in our mathematics. Then, for the South Pole dipole RF antennas used in detection, the response function is:

\begin{equation}
r(t) = R_0 \cos(2\pi f_0 t) e^{-2\pi\gamma t} u(t) \label{eq:2}
\end{equation}
The task is to convolve $r(t)$ and $s(t)$. A strategy derived from Digital Signal Processing.  For the case where $t<0$, the convolution is:

\begin{multline}
s * r = \\ E_0 R_0 \Re\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{\omega_0t}e^{-\omega_0\tau}e^{2\pi jf_0\tau}e^{-2\pi\gamma\tau}u(\tau)d\tau
\end{multline}

Factor the terms out of the integral that do not involve $\tau$ and account for the Heavyside step function:

\begin{multline}
s * r = \\ E_0 R_0 \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{\omega_0t}\Re\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\omega_0\tau}e^{2\pi jf_0\tau}e^{-2\pi\gamma\tau}d\tau
\\ = E_0 R_0 \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{\omega_0t}\Re\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\omega_0+2\pi\gamma-2\pi jf_0)\tau}d\tau
\end{multline}

The Heavyside step function here ensures our third integral does not diverge such that the complex result for $t<0$ is

\begin{equation}
s * r =  E_0 R_0 \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)\Re((\omega_0+2\pi\gamma-2\pi jf_0)^{-1})e^{\omega_0t}
\end{equation}

The result is a rising exponential ($t<0$) that does not oscillate at the response frequency $f_0$.  Let $r_a$ be the analytic signal for $r(t)$, and let $s_+(t)$ and $s_-(t)$ be the piecewise branches of $s(t)$.  For the case where $t\ge 0$, we have

\begin{multline}
s * r = \\ E_0 R_0 \Re \left( \int_{-\infty}^t s_+(t-\tau) r_a(\tau) d\tau + \int_t^{\infty} s_-(t-\tau) r_a(\tau) d\tau\right)
\end{multline}

Using Eqs. \ref{eq:1} and \ref{eq:2}, we find

\begin{multline}
s * r = E_0 R_0 \Re \left( 2e^{-2\omega_C t}\int_0^t e^{2\omega_C \tau}r_a(\tau)d\tau \right. \\ - \left. \left(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{-\omega_0 t}\int_0^{t} e^{\omega_0 \tau} r_a(\tau)d\tau \right. \\ + \left. \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{\omega_0 t}\int_t^{\infty} e^{-\omega_0 \tau} r_a(\tau) d\tau \right)
\end{multline}

Define the following constants, with units of frequency:

\begin{align}
z_1 &= 2\omega_C + 2\pi jf_0 -2\pi\gamma \\
z_2 &= \omega_0 + 2\pi jf_0 -2\pi\gamma \\
z_3 &= -\omega_0 + 2\pi jf_0 -2\pi\gamma
\end{align}

Using these definitions and Eq. \ref{eq:2}, the convolution becomes

\begin{multline}
s * r = E_0 R_0 \Re \left( 2e^{-2\omega_C t}\int_0^t e^{z_1 \tau}d\tau \right. \\ - \left. \left(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{-\omega_0 t}\int_0^{t} e^{z_2 \tau}d\tau\right. \\ + \left. \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{\omega_0 t}\int_t^{\infty} e^{z_3 \tau}d\tau \right)
\end{multline}

Evaluating the integrals and distributing the $\Re$ operator:

\begin{multline}
s * r = E_0 R_0 \left( 2 e^{-2\omega_C t} \Re\left( z_1^{-1} e^{z_1 t} - z  _1^{-1}\right) \right. \\ \left. - \left(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{-\omega_0 t} \Re\left( z_2^{-1} e^{z_2 t} - z_2^{-1}\right) \right. \\ \left. - \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)e^{\omega_0 t} \Re\left(z_3^{-1} e^{z_3 t}\right) \right)
\end{multline}

In order to write out the real result of the integral, let us accept three common rules from complex analysis. The first being that 
the real part of a complex number $z$ is:

\begin{equation}
\Re(z) = \frac{z+z^*}{2}
\end{equation}

Where $z^*$ denotes the complex conjugate. And for our current abstract case, $z = \alpha+j\beta$.  The second rule we must accept is that the inverse of a complex number is: 

\begin{equation}
z^{-1} = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{z^*}{zz^*} = \frac{\alpha-j\beta}{\alpha^2+\beta^2}
\end{equation}

The third rule in our complex analysis we must derive from our rules of exponents and Euler's identity. Recall:

\begin{multline}
 Euler's \ identity: \ e^{i\pi} + 1= 0
\end{multline}

We will treat the components of z, $\alpha$ + i$\beta$, as separate factors such that
\begin{equation}
e^{z} = e^{\alpha+i{\beta}} = e^{\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{\alpha}cos(\beta)-isin(\beta)
\end{equation}

It follows then, that if we follow these rules for every term for which we want to take the real component. We arrive to: 
\begin{equation}
 \Re( z_n^{-1} e^{z_n t} - z_n^{-1}) = \frac{\alpha_n}{\alpha_n^2+\beta_n^2}e^{\alpha_n t}cos(\beta t)) - \frac{\alpha_n}{\alpha_n^2+\beta_n^2}   
\end{equation}
The third term operated on by $\Re$ has a sub-term which tends to infinity, so we do not observe a subtracted value. And the Heavyside-Step function is applied -as before. Such that the real part of the convolution for an on-cone $t\ge0$ signal is:

\begin{multline}
s * r = E_0R_0(2e^{-2\omega_ct}(\frac{\alpha_{z_1}}{\alpha_{z_1}^2+\beta_{z_1}^2}e^{\alpha_{z_1}t}cos(\beta_{z_1}t)- \frac{\alpha_{z_1}}{\alpha_{z_1}^2+\beta_{z_1}^2}) \\
 -(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)e^{-\omega_0t}(\frac{\alpha_{z_2}}{\alpha_{z_2}^2+\beta_{z_2}^2}e^{\alpha_{z_2}t}cos(\beta_{z_2}t)- \frac{\alpha_{z_2}}{\alpha_{z_2}^2+\beta_{z_2}^2}) \\ + (1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon)e^{\omega_0t}(\frac{\alpha_{z_3}}{\alpha_{z_3}^2+\beta_{z_3}^2}e^{\alpha_{z_3}t}cos(\beta_{z_3}t))
\end{multline}


\section{Reconstruction}
\label{sec:Rec}
With our \textit{on-cone} equations solved for, we may closely reconstruct a UHECR candidate event using an algorithm, and known open access candidate event's data. Recall equation eq.5 and eq.18, the final results for $t<0$ and $t\ge0$. In order for our reconstruction to be sufficiently accurate, we must treat these functions as a piecewise function to model the overall time-lapse of the event -albeit there is of course no 'negative' time, $t<0$. The 'negative' time arises from the RF antenna interpretation of $t=0$ being the peak of the Askaryan induced cascade. This must be accounted for in the code. Also, by following similar steps in \ref{sec:Der}, Eq 5 also yields a real solution. The algebra is trivial and is best left as an exercise to the reader. Our algorithm then uses our final piecewise solution to parse through the frequency parameters, $\omega_0$ and $\omega_c$.

The data set for the UHECR event candidates was made possible by the ARA collaboration through a data release [zenodo]. Assumptions of the ARA detector are made in that; \newline
1. Detector calibration pulses closely match actual cosmic ray events \newline

The data file is imported into the code and cross correlated with the piecewise model. When $\rho$ is maximized, per event ID, the best fit values are printed.
